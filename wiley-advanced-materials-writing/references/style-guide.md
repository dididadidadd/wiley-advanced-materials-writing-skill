# Sample-Derived Style Guide

This guide summarizes patterns extracted from representative Advanced Materials / Advanced Functional Materials papers. It should guide structure and voice, not produce copied language.

## Representative Pattern Families

- Anti-corrosion superhydrophobic epoxy coatings.
- Hydrophobic resin anti-corrosion and self-healing coatings.
- Repairable superhydrophobic anti-icing surfaces.
- Oleogel microsphere-based composite protective coatings.
- Uncoated superhydrophobic anti-icing elastomer or propeller-edge surfaces.

## Shared Abstract Arc

The sampled articles usually follow this chain:

1. A high-stakes application field is named immediately: maritime corrosion, coating damage, aircraft/propeller icing, harsh outdoor service, or long-term protection.
2. The limitation is practical and specific: poor compatibility, insufficient durability, slow fabrication, high processing cost, coating instability, or self-healing/stability tradeoff.
3. The design strategy is named as an architecture, process, or synergy: interfacial strengthening cells, hydrophobic solvent plus elastic resin, templating plus flame induction, soft-hard hybrid architecture, or laser-induced ceramization.
4. Performance is quantified with conditions: contact angle, sliding angle, immersion time, healing time, abrasion/corrosion/UV duration, anti-icing delay, electrochemical stability, or recovery time.
5. Mechanism is assigned to structure-property relationships: micro/nanostructure, surface chemistry, compact barrier, confined flow, elastic recovery, ceramization, diffusion path extension, or interfacial compatibility.
6. The final sentence frames the work as a viable strategy for durable protective materials, not as a broad generic advance.

## Introduction Pattern

Strong introductions use a funnel:

- Start from a concrete application pressure rather than a universal opening.
- Briefly map the existing strategy landscape.
- State why existing approaches have reached a bottleneck.
- Introduce the proposed design principle and why it can address that bottleneck.
- Preview the material design and the main evidence chain.

Avoid beginning with broad claims such as "materials are important to modern society." Prefer a field-specific problem:

- corrosion causes premature failure and economic loss in marine or industrial environments;
- coating defects expose substrates and accelerate localized corrosion;
- icing on propeller leading edges or aircraft components creates safety risks;
- durable superwetting surfaces require both robust microstructure and stable surface chemistry.

## Preferred Claim Shapes

Use these shapes as templates, filling them with the user's real facts:

- "However, [specific limitation] remains a major challenge because [mechanistic/practical reason]."
- "Herein, we report/design/develop [material architecture] by [fabrication or chemical strategy]."
- "The resulting [sample] achieves [metric] under [condition], while maintaining [secondary property] after [durability test]."
- "This performance arises from the synergy between [component/structure A] and [component/structure B], which [mechanism]."
- "These results establish [strategy] as a promising route toward [specific application]."

## Materials-Coating Vocabulary

Useful verbs:

- enables, affords, maintains, suppresses, delays, confines, reinforces, stabilizes, recovers, extends, promotes, mitigates, preserves, resists, decouples, integrates.

Useful nouns:

- architecture, interface, micro/nanostructure, barrier, diffusion pathway, compatibility, durability, self-healing, robustness, wettability, surface chemistry, coating matrix, network, substrate, failure mode.

Use with care:

- "synergistic" only when the experiment separates the roles of two factors.
- "robust" only with abrasion, UV, chemical, immersion, water-jet, bending, or long-cycle evidence.
- "long-term" only with time or cycle data.
- "autonomous self-healing" only without external stimulus.

## Section-Specific Guidance

### Abstract

Keep it as a single dense paragraph. The most AM/AFM-like abstracts put the strongest data in the middle, then explain the mechanism.

Recommended sentence count: 6-8 sentences.

### Results

Each subsection should have a claim-driven first sentence. Do not start every paragraph with "Figure X shows". Use figure references after the claim.

Good flow:

1. "To verify [design feature], [characterization] was performed."
2. "Compared with [control], [sample] shows [difference]."
3. "The [result] indicates [chemical/structural interpretation]."
4. "Together, these results confirm [claim]."

### Figure Captions

Captions in this style are factual and compact. Include:

- panel identity;
- sample names;
- test conditions;
- key variable or metric;
- visual/experimental takeaway.

Avoid promotional adjectives in captions.

## Chinese-To-English Conversion Rules

- Move the main claim to the beginning of the English sentence.
- Split long Chinese logic chains into shorter English claim/mechanism pairs.
- Convert "because..., therefore..." into "This behavior is attributed to..." or "This improvement arises from...".
- Translate "synergistic effect" or the Chinese phrase for synergy as "synergy" only when both components are experimentally justified; otherwise use "combined effect".
- Translate generic "improved/enhanced" wording with the measured property: "improves corrosion resistance", "reduces water adhesion", "extends the diffusion pathway", or "enhances barrier performance".

## Checklist Before Finalizing

- Does the section state the unresolved problem before the proposed strategy?
- Are all major claims tied to data, controls, or mechanisms?
- Are performance values accompanied by conditions and sample names?
- Is the novelty framed as a design principle rather than a slogan?
- Does the prose avoid unsupported hype?
- Are mechanism words consistent across abstract, results, conclusion, and captions?
